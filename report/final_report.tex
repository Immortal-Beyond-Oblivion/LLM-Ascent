\begin{abstract}
Project Ascent builds decoder-only Transformer language models from scratch and
measures what each design decision actually changes. The models are deliberately
tiny --- 1.5M, 2.6M and 6.3M non-embedding parameters at a 128-token context ---
and this paper reports the negative results with the same rigour as the positive
ones. Three findings carry the paper, and Table~\ref{tab:findings} is the
taxonomy of everything else: 89 rows in four populations, 10 \emph{supported},
26 \emph{unsupported}, 15 \emph{measurement failures} and 38
\emph{declined claims}, every row traceable to a tracked file. The
\textbf{first finding is the calibration result}. Over 4,064 held-out
next-token predictions the model is accurate $52.95\%$ of the time at a mean
confidence of $52.55\%$, with an expected calibration error of $0.0233$ and
bins that track (confidence $0.979 \rightarrow$ accuracy $0.962$; confidence
$0.054 \rightarrow$ accuracy $0.074$). The model is a competent, well-calibrated
next-token predictor and an incompetent instruction follower, grounder and tool
user, and every downstream failure in this paper is a failure to \emph{use} the
competence rather than a failure to have it. The \textbf{second finding is
that instruction tuning retargets that competence rather than adding to it}.
Tuning on 512 examples raises held-out instruction following from $0.0000$ to
$0.6016$ while adding no next-token competence on the distribution it was tuned
on: there the accuracy difference is $-0.0009$ (95\% $[-0.0160, +0.0142]$, not
significant), the mean confidence nevertheless rises from $0.3969$ to $0.6261$
with the accuracy flat, and the calibration error on that distribution more
than doubles ($0.1118 \rightarrow 0.2772$). Off target the trade is paid in
accuracy: the tuned model loses 10.7 points of next-token accuracy
($-0.1068$, 95\% $[-0.1284, -0.0852]$) and its calibration error rises from
$0.0233$ to $0.1306$. The \textbf{third finding is the contamination}. The
scaling ladder mixed two architecture families, was recorded on a parameter
axis the published exponents do not use, and was trained by a pipeline that
was never in the repository --- so a mid-campaign change of model class was
invisible to every downstream artefact, including the ledger --- and its
headline exponent is withdrawn. The study of what replaced it says that this
ladder is \emph{informative about $\beta$ and uninformative about $\alpha$}.
$E$ is the conditional entropy of an infinite model, so the corpus's unigram
entropy of $4.9946$ nats is a loose ceiling and never a target; the correct
anchors are the token-space compression ceiling of $3.5918$ nats per token and
the model's own achieved held-out loss of $2.5152$ nats, and $E$ must lie
below the achieved loss. Both fitted $E$ values do --- $1.964$ on the
total-parameter axis and $1.911$ on the non-embedding axis --- so the bound
admits both conventions; the axis the paper uses is the published one, and the
conclusion is unchanged. The $\beta$ profile's compatible range
$[0.38,\ 0.54]$ excludes Kaplan's $0.30$ and contains Chinchilla's $0.50$; the
$\alpha$ profile's range $[0.34,\ 0.82]$ contains both published values and
discriminates between none of them. The $\alpha$ width is not noise: it is a
ridge, with $d\alpha/dE = +0.5542$. The refit is not a minor correction.
The withdrawn fit's own parameters score $R^2 = +0.9957$ on the other family's
points and $-1.312$ on the canonical models' own points, and that other family
owned the entire top of the ladder, so the original ``consistent with
Chinchilla'' claim was resting on it. A KV cache gives byte-identical output
and 3.71$\times$ to 18.36$\times$; sharing KV heads buys an 8$\times$ cache
reduction and 12.1\% fewer FLOPs at a small but statistically significant
quality cost; speculative decoding is a bounded negative whose cause is
arithmetic rather than implementation. Decoding is a clean quality/diversity
trade-off whose quality axis is self-preferring, and self-consistency cannot
manufacture competence that is absent. Supervised fine-tuning scales
monotonically with no saturation in range, and in-context learning copies a
demonstration's \emph{format} without its operation. On grounding, the model
identifies which number a passage asserts \emph{at chance}, and the logit lens
shows why: context numbers are represented in the residual stream and
assertion-specific content is not, so the representation is not carried to the
output. That act's control set was run after the claim was written, it went
\emph{against} the stronger version of the claim, and the stronger version is
withdrawn here rather than quietly dropped. The lesson we want remembered: a
test that compares two implementations cannot detect a bug that both of them
share, so prefer an absolute sanity invariant --- and check the provenance of
your data set before you fit a law to it. A good-looking fit is not evidence
of a good fit. All fifteen measurement failures this project recorded were
caught by such an invariant or by a positive control, and not one of them by
comparing two implementations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and research questions}
\label{sec:intro}

This is a course project in building a language model from scratch and
\emph{measuring} the effect of every design choice, rather than asserting it.
The models are intentionally small: the largest has 6.3M non-embedding
parameters and a 128-token context. That scale cannot demonstrate useful
capability, and this paper does not claim that it does. What the scale can do
is isolate effects, and several of the effects it isolates are null results with
a mechanism attached.

The project is organised as ten \emph{acts}. Each act removes one limitation
that a previous act exposed, and each act ends in the same shape: a table with
its uncertainty stated, one or two figures, and a conclusion that says both what
the measurement supports and what it does not. A negative result gets a table,
figures and a conclusion, exactly as a positive one does. There is no section
in this paper that reports a number and moves on.

\textbf{The thesis, stated by the measurement that carries it.} The models
built here are competent, well-calibrated next-token predictors and
incompetent instruction followers, grounders and tool users. Over 4,064
held-out next-token predictions on C4, the 6.3M model is accurate $52.95\%$ of
the time at a mean confidence of $52.55\%$, with an expected calibration error
of $0.0233$ and confidence bins that track accuracy closely (confidence
$0.979 \rightarrow$ accuracy $0.962$; confidence $0.054 \rightarrow$ accuracy
$0.074$). That is the only positive result in the act that measures it, and
every other act in this paper is a measurement of a failure to \emph{use} that
competence: a zero-shot instruction floor at exactly 0.0000, a grounding rate
at chance, a tool-call template whose argument is always \texttt{s}, an
in-context learner that copies a demonstration's format and not its operation.
A failure to use a competence is a different kind of fact from a failure to
have one, and this project's contribution is to separate the two. The
consequence for the reader is a specific and slightly uncomfortable one: the
floor results in Acts 7, 8 and 9 are not evidence that the models cannot do
those things in principle, and they are not evidence that they can either.
They are evidence that at this scale, with this training budget, on this
corpus, the capability that is present does not travel. % claims: A10C-03

\textbf{The second-largest result is a negative one, and it is about the act
the project was built around.} The scaling ladder's exponent turned out to be
unidentifiable at this scale, and the reasons are now known in detail rather
than guessed at. The ladder mixed two architecture families because the
training pipeline that produced one of them was never committed to the
repository, so a mid-campaign change of model class was invisible to every
downstream artefact including the ledger. What replaced the withdrawn fit is
an \textbf{asymmetry}: on the total-parameter axis the ladder is
\emph{informative about $\beta$ and uninformative about $\alpha$}. The
compatible $\beta$ range is $[0.38,\ 0.54]$ --- it excludes Kaplan's $0.30$
and contains Chinchilla's $0.50$, so it discriminates --- while the compatible
$\alpha$ range is $[0.34,\ 0.82]$, wide enough to contain every published
exponent, so \textbf{on $\alpha$ the ladder is compatible across their entire
range and discriminates between none of them}. The mechanism is the design of
the sweep: each run contributes interior checkpoints at fixed $N$ and rising
$D$, so within-run $D$ variation pins $\beta$, while $\alpha$ depends on the
number and spread of distinct $N$ values, of which the ladder now has eight. The
$\alpha$ width is not noise but a ridge: fixing $E$ on a grid and re-optimising
$\alpha$ traces a monotone path with $d\alpha/dE = +0.5542$. Both results are
reported in Section~\ref{sec:act4}. % claims: A4-09, A4-18,
% claims: A4-19

The research questions, in order, are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Do the classical baselines behave as advertised?} (Acts 0--1.)
      How much does n-gram order matter relative to smoothing, and are
      recurrent architectures distinguishable at all on a corpus this small?
\item \textbf{Which Transformer components actually matter?} (Acts 2--3.)
      What do normalisation, positional encoding, head count and FFN activation
      do when measured one seed at a time, and how much of a tokenizer
      comparison survives a context control?
\item \textbf{Do the published scaling laws hold below their fitted range?}
      (Act 4.) We fit our own $\alpha$ and $\beta$, on the parameter axis the
      published exponents use, and report the profile likelihood rather than a
      point estimate: on $\alpha$ the ladder is compatible with published
      exponents across their entire range and discriminates between none of
      them, while $\beta$, which is two to three times better constrained,
      excludes one published value and contains the other. We also ask whether
      the fitted $E$ is a measured entropy; it is not, it is an extrapolated
      asymptote, and the width of the $\alpha$ profile turns out to be an
      $(E, \alpha)$ ridge rather than noise.
\item \textbf{What do inference-time techniques buy, and what do they cost?}
      (Acts 5--6.) KV caching, shared KV heads, speculative decoding, and the
      decoding sweep, with quality measured against a fixed judge.
\item \textbf{Can a model this small be taught to follow instructions, and does
      it ground what it reads?} (Acts 7--8.) Supervised fine-tuning,
      in-context learning, parameter-efficient adaptation, retrieval-augmented
      answering and tool use.
\item \textbf{Where does the information go, and can the project audit
      itself?} (Acts 9--10.) Causal head ablation, attention attribution and
      a logit lens, each with the control that decides whether its claim
      survives; then the measurement-audit ledger and the taxonomy of the ways
      a measurement in this project failed to measure what it claimed to.
\end{enumerate}

The answer, in one sentence per act, is in Sections 3--13. The taxonomy of
every hypothesis the project put at risk --- with the four populations, their
counts and the source of every row --- is Table~\ref{tab:findings}, at the front
of this paper and generated from one tracked file
(\texttt{results/v2/findings\_taxonomy.json}). The headline claims are
tabulated with their $n$, their interval and their status in
Section~\ref{sec:act10a}, the full per-claim ledger is the tracked artefact
\texttt{report/claims.csv}, and Section~\ref{sec:dnm} publishes the claims we
deliberately do \emph{not} make.

\subsection{Data and tokenization}
\label{sec:method-data}

Three corpora are used, and results are never pooled across them. (i) TinyShakespeare
(1,115,394 characters, 66-symbol vocabulary) is the corpus for Acts 0--3, with
a 90/10 character-level train/validation split of 1,003,854 / 111,540
characters. (ii) A C4 English shard (356,317 documents, 769,299,382 characters,
SHA-256 recorded in the corpus manifest) is the corpus for Acts 4--9; it is
tokenised with a 6,487-token BPE fitted on a 50,025,065-character sample of the
training split only, at 2.2726 characters per token. (iii) Two held-out
evaluation sets that are never trained on: MNLI (300 items, used in
Section~\ref{sec:act6}) and a synthetic instruction family with 512 training
and 128 held-out items (used in Section~\ref{sec:act7}).

Two tokenizer corrections belong to the method, not to an act, because they
changed what a measurement could mean. Tokenizers are now fitted on the
\emph{training} split only, and validation is computed by a seeded full sweep
rather than by unseeded random batches. Both fixes were needed before any
v2 number could be trusted.

\subsection{Measurement protocol}
\label{sec:method-protocol}

Every number in this paper carries its sample size, its interval and its status.
The rules are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Seeds.} A claim that is meant to establish an effect is measured
      with $n \geq 3$ seeds and reported as mean $\pm$ sample standard
      deviation. Where a method is deterministic, the three ``seeds'' are
      bit-identical; that is stated, and the result is relabelled $n=1$ in
      substance rather than being given a zero-width error bar.
\item \textbf{Accuracy.} Proportions are reported with 95\% Wilson score
      intervals. Perplexity, loss and log-probability contrasts are reported as
      mean $\pm$ standard deviation or as a paired difference with a bootstrap
      interval; a Wilson interval is never used for a continuous quantity.
\item \textbf{Unit of analysis.} Where a claim is about an item, the item is
      the unit. Repeat generations of the same item are averaged per item
      before scoring, and contrast intervals resample \emph{items}, because
      repeats of an item share its difficulty and are not independent draws.
      This distinction changes a Wilson bound: $0/40$ items gives an upper
      bound of 0.0876, not the 0.031 that a spurious $n = 120$ would give.
\item \textbf{Controls before comparisons.} Every A-versus-B experiment ships
      with the control that makes the comparison mean something: an
      equal-character-span control for tokenizer work, a non-numeric passage
      control for grounding, a matched trainable-parameter budget for
      parameter-efficient adaptation, and a zero-gap configuration for
      speculative decoding.
\item \textbf{Pre-registration.} A falsification condition is written down
      before the run that it judges. Act~4's condition was written on
      2026-10-15 and is reproduced verbatim in Section~\ref{sec:act4}. Anything
      run after a result was seen is labelled \emph{exploratory}, and no
      exploratory result is presented as a headline. The convention for the
      whole project, and the acts that did not follow it, are stated in
      Section~\ref{sec:method-falsification}.
\item \textbf{Status vocabulary.} \emph{confirmed} (the measurement supports
      the claim at the stated $n$ and interval), \emph{n.s.} (measured, and the
      interval does not exclude the null), \emph{exploratory} (single seed, or
      run after seeing results), \emph{ceiling} (the metric is saturated, so the
      value cannot rank conditions), \emph{invalid} (the measurement does not
      test what it claims to test), \emph{superseded} (a later measurement
      replaces it).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The falsification convention, and where it was not followed}
\label{sec:method-falsification}

The convention this project holds itself to is that \textbf{every act should
have a written falsifier before the run it judges}: a statement of what would
have counted against the claim, written down while it is still cheap to be
wrong. Where such a document exists, this paper names it. Where one does not,
Table~\ref{tab:findings} says so, row by row, in its \emph{preregistered}
column, and the corresponding row of the tracked artefact
(\texttt{results/v2/findings\_taxonomy.json}) carries the note.

The honest accounting is this. \textbf{Exactly one act in this project has a
written falsifier}: Act~4, whose condition is
\texttt{results/v2/act4-scaling/FALSIFICATION\_CONDITION.md}, dated 2026-10-15
and reproduced in Section~\ref{sec:act4-falsification}. It was written before
any run of the sweep, it is the document that makes Act~4's headline
"exploratory" rather than "confirmed" in the places where the family
separation and the axis convention were decided after the fact, and it is the
row in Table~\ref{tab:findings} whose \emph{preregistered} column reads
\emph{true}. Every other act's hypothesis row reads \emph{false}.

\textbf{And the most decisive control in the project was post-hoc.} Act~7's
zero-shot floor is assertable only because a positive control --- an oracle
emitting the correct answer, scored through the teacher-forced path, the
greedy path and the exact in-context scoring path --- scored $1.0$ while the
real model scored $0.0000$. That control was run \emph{after} the $0.0000$
floor had been recorded, and it turned out to be the thing that made the
floor assertable at all. The same-task control behind Act~7b was built after
the first in-context measurement had been voided by truncation. Neither is
marked as pre-registered, because neither was: they are marked
\emph{post-hoc} in Table~\ref{tab:findings} and in the ledger, which is more
honest than pretending they were planned.

Two consequences follow, and both are limits rather than boasts. First, this
project cannot claim pre-registration as a general practice: of the
hypotheses in Table~\ref{tab:findings}, one was falsifier-first. Second, the
project's actual defence is not pre-registration at all but the taxonomy in
Section~\ref{sec:act10b}: every measurement failure that was \emph{recorded}
was caught by an absolute invariant or a positive control, and the controls
that were decisive are marked as post-hoc where they were post-hoc.

Table~\ref{tab:findings} uses a four-value status vocabulary of its own, which
is deliberately coarser than the ledger's: \emph{supported} (the hypothesis
held), \emph{unsupported} (it did not --- the negative-result population),
\emph{measurement failure} (a defect that was caught and documented) and
\emph{declined claim} (a claim this paper does not make). The ledger in
Section~\ref{sec:act10a} uses a different and finer vocabulary
(\emph{confirmed}, \emph{n.s.}, \emph{exploratory}, \emph{ceiling},
\emph{invalid}, \emph{superseded}, \emph{open-item}) because a ledger row
describes the evidential status of a \emph{number}, while a taxonomy row
describes the fate of a \emph{hypothesis}.

\subsection{A note on what a 6.3M model can and cannot do}
\label{sec:method-scale}

It is worth being explicit about the scale, because several results are only
interpretable against it. A 6.3M-parameter model trained on 125.8M tokens
reaches a validation loss of 2.5152 nats, or roughly 3.63 bits per token. It
is a competent next-token predictor of web text and an incompetent instruction
follower, because instruction following is not a next-token prediction problem
at this scale. It can copy the surface form of a demonstration while failing
the operation the demonstration performs, and it can emit a tool-call template
while being unable to fill it. Several of the negative results in this paper
(Sections~\ref{sec:act7}, \ref{sec:act8} and \ref{sec:act9}) are not failures
of method; they are the correct measurement of a model that has learned a
format and not a capability --- and Act~9 measures where that information goes:
it holds a generic number representation in the residual stream, and that
representation does not reach the output. Reporting any of them as something
other than what was measured would be the easy mistake, and this paper tries
not to make it.

\subsection{Embeddings: built, not measured}
\label{sec:act0-embed}

The syllabus for this act also lists dense representations: truncated SVD of a
count matrix, word2vec in both CBOW and skip-gram form with negative sampling
and frequent-word subsampling, GloVe, and fastText with character n-grams and
boundary symbols. \textbf{None of this work is implemented in this repository
and none of it is measured.} There is no \texttt{src/embed} package, no tracked
result file and no figure. % claims: A0-03
 We therefore report it as absent rather than
dressing up the absence as a result, and the coverage checklist in
\texttt{AGENTS.md} records it as an open item.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:act0-conclusion}

\textbf{What the measurement supports.} Order dominates smoothing. Moving from
order 1 to order 3 reduces validation perplexity from 28.43 to 7.45--8.78, a
factor of 3.2--3.8, while the spread across the four smoothing schemes at a
fixed order is at most 0.9 in perplexity at order 3 and is exactly zero at
order 1. The best configuration in the table is trigram Kneser--Ney at 7.446
perplexity (2.897 bits per character).

\textbf{What it does not support.} It does not support a claim about smoothing
methods in general, because the comparison is single-corpus, single-split and
deterministic, and because the interpolation arm is handicapped by construction:
its weights are fixed at (0.1, 0.3, 0.6) for every order, so at order 3 it is
the only arm that is being asked to interpolate a distribution it was not tuned
for, and it duly comes last (8.778). We do not claim that interpolation is a
worse smoothing scheme; we claim that the arm as configured is the worst of the
four here, which is a statement about the configuration. % claims: A0-01, A0-02
 The three ``seeds''
give no uncertainty estimate at all, and the table is presented with the
standard deviation of 0.000 visible for exactly that reason.

\section{Act 1: recurrent baselines}
\label{sec:act1}

\subsection{Setup and results}
\label{sec:act1-results}

A plain RNN, an LSTM and a GRU are trained on the same character-level
TinyShakespeare split as Act 0 (500 steps, batch size 16, context 64), three
seeds each, with parameter counts matched only loosely.

\subsection{The gradient demonstration, which is a null}
\label{sec:act1-grad}

The experiment was built to demonstrate vanishing gradients, and it does not.
The total gradient norm over all parameters rises over the 100 steps of the copy
task: from 0.4363 to 0.4829 for the plain RNN, and from 0.1412 to 0.2828 for
the LSTM (means 0.4400 and 0.1803 over the trace). The LSTM's norms are
\emph{lower} than the RNN's throughout, which is the opposite of the ordering the
gating argument predicts. At sequence length 20 on a copy task, vanishing
gradients were not demonstrated at all. We report the measurement, not the
narrative the experiment was designed to tell.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:act1-conclusion}

\textbf{What the measurement supports.} RNN, LSTM and GRU are
\emph{indistinguishable} at $n = 3$ on this corpus. The largest gap, LSTM minus
RNN, is 0.152 nats, which is smaller than the smaller of the two standard
deviations (0.215 and 0.285); the LSTM's mean is also worse than both
alternatives, but not by an amount the seed spread does not already explain.

\textbf{What it does not support.} It does not support the ordering that
recurrent architectures are usually reported in, and it does not support a
general claim that gates do not help. The comparison is confounded by capacity:
the LSTM carries 58,434 parameters against the RNN's 31,362, so the LSTM is
being asked to do more with the same 500 steps and the same 1.1M characters. A
parameter-matched comparison at longer training would be needed to say anything
about gates, and we do not make that claim. Nor does this act demonstrate
vanishing gradients: the demonstration measured the opposite, at a sequence
length far too short to produce the effect. % claims: A1-01, A1-02

\subsection{Why the numbers in this section are a ranking and not an effect}
\label{sec:act2-n1}

At $n = 1$ there is no variance estimate, so no comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:act2} can be tested. The differences between configurations
range over 0.179 nats across a set that is likely to have a seed spread of the
same order as the smallest differences shown, and we have no measurement of
that spread. Accordingly this section contains no $\pm$ columns, no $p$-values
and no comparative adjectives except the word ``suggests'', which is used
nowhere without it. The rows are reported so that a reader can see the shape of
the ranking, and so that a future multi-seed rerun has a baseline to be
compared against. % claims: A2-01


\subsection{The ALiBi arm did not measure ALiBi}
\label{sec:act2-alibi}

The two lowest rows of the positional-encoding block are not two results;
they are one result counted twice, and we would rather say so than let the
table imply a finding. The \texttt{pe-alibi} and \texttt{pe-none}
configurations differ in exactly one field (\texttt{positional\_encoding}), have
identical parameter counts (104,320), and produce final validation losses of
2.4850885868 and 2.4850885391 --- a difference of $4.8\times10^{-8}$ nats, which
is float32 round-off. Their loss trajectories agree to the same precision at
every logged step.

The cause is in the source, not in the measurement.
\texttt{src/model/ablations.py} defines \texttt{ALiBiPE.forward} to return its
input unchanged, with the comment that the attention bias is applied in
attention, and the ablation model's forward pass carries the comment
\emph{``ALiBi: apply attention bias (not implemented, skip)''}. The ALiBi arm
therefore ran the no-positional-encoding model. The honest reading of
Table~\ref{tab:act2} is that \emph{no positional encoding scored best among
the encodings that were actually implemented}, and that ALiBi was not measured
at all. The claim ``ALiBi is competitive with learned absolute positions'' is
\textbf{invalid}; the claim ``ALiBi was not measured'' is confirmed. % claims: A2-02
 This is
the same class of error as the Act~5 beam-cache claim in
Section~\ref{sec:act5-methodology} and as Act~7's defect D11: an assertion
about what the code does, with no test behind it.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:act2-conclusion}

\textbf{What the measurement supports.} A single-seed ranking of 18
configurations. Post-norm LayerNorm (2.5615) and post-norm RMSNorm (2.5647)
occupy the top of the normalisation block, BatchNorm occupies the bottom
(2.6610--2.6637), SwiGLU leads the FFN block (2.5725, at 32,128 extra
parameters), and 4 heads beat 2 beat 1 in the head block. The absence of
positional encoding leads the encodings that were implemented.

\textbf{What it does not support.} None of the above as an \emph{effect}. It
does not support any statement about ALiBi, RoPE, sinusoidal or learned
positioning, because there is no variance estimate and one of the five arms did
not run the thing it names. It does not support the claim that post-norm beats
pre-norm, or that SwiGLU beats GELU, or that more heads are better; at $n = 1$
these are suggestions. And it does not support any comparison with the other
acts in this paper, because the metric here is validation loss on a
character-level corpus at a 64-token context, which is a different scale from
every other act.

\subsection{Conclusion, and what happened to the v1 headline}
\label{sec:act3-conclusion}

\textbf{What the measurement supports.} Under the equal-count control, Unigram
LM is the strongest tokenizer at 2.8021 bits per character, followed by BPE at
3.0801, then character at 3.4680 and WordPiece at 3.4998. The equal-span
control moves every tokenizer slightly and moves BPE most (3.0801 to 3.1893),
which is the expected direction: shortening BPE's window from 64 tokens to 39
tokens costs it context, and it still beats the character model. WordPiece,
whose tokens carry essentially one character, behaves like the character model
and slightly worse, which is the expected direction as well.

\textbf{What it does not support.} It does not support the v1 headline, and
the v1 headline is not reported here as a current result. The v1 comparison
recorded 3.4525 bits per character for the character model against 3.0946 for
BPE --- about 10\% lower for BPE --- and it carried four self-declared confounds:
a context confound (BPE saw more text per window), BPE merges fitted on the
validation text, validation loss computed from 20 unseeded random batches, and
a single seed with a single merge count. Act~3 supersedes that comparison
structurally: it has the context control that removes confound (i) and a
seeded full sweep that removes confound (iii). But Act~3 is \emph{itself} run
at a single seed, so it replaces a confounded single-seed result with a
controlled single-seed result. The ordering of the two tokenizer families in
Act 3 is the same as in v1 (BPE ahead of character), and the Unigram result is
new. No claim about tokenizer families is established at $n = 1$. % claims: A3-01, A3-02, A3-03


\section{Act 4: scaling laws}
\label{sec:act4}

\subsection{Method: one run, dozens of points}
\label{sec:act4-method}

The sweep is a ladder over model size, trained on the C4 shard with the
6,487-token BPE described in Section~\ref{sec:method-data}. The method
that made the act affordable is worth stating plainly, because it is the most
transferable thing in the paper: \textbf{a training run is not one data point}.
Every run logs its validation loss against tokens seen at each evaluation
interval, so a single run yields dozens of $(N, D, \mathrm{loss})$ points along
a fixed-$N$ curve. The ladder is 23 runs over eight rungs; restricting to the
interior region $D \geq 5$M leaves 5,176 $(N, D, \mathrm{loss})$ points, of
which 3,463 belong to the canonical family the fit uses --- against the
twenty-three endpoint values that a naive ``one point per model'' reading
would give. The parameter axis those points are recorded on
was itself a defect, and Section~\ref{sec:act4-axis} settles it; the
homogeneity of the model family those points belong to was a second defect,
and Section~\ref{sec:act4-cause} explains it.
% claims: A4-07

\subsection{The ladder was two architecture families, not one}
\label{sec:act4-provenance}

Reading the run records rather than the ledger, the ladder is not one model
family. Fourteen of the twenty-five run records are the canonical
\texttt{MiniGPT} --- 8 heads, learned absolute positional encoding ---
confirmed from the surviving checkpoints' state dicts and from the
\texttt{architecture} block that the training code writes into every run's
\texttt{metrics.json}. Five are a different family, \texttt{alibi\_like}, with
\emph{no} learned position table. The two are trivially separable by the first
logged validation loss, because the other family has no position table to
learn: the canonical runs start at 8.72--8.91 nats, the other family at
129.57--223.77 nats. The remaining six records are excluded from every fit:
three runs of a third architecture, which no
$(\text{dim}, \text{layers}, \text{heads})$ combination reproduces, at the
0.8M rung; two ``tiny'' runs that are not rungs at all; and one 1.5M run
superseded by the rescue of Section~\ref{sec:act4-rescue}. The
separation is not delicate, and it agrees with the architecture block wherever
that block exists.

The two families do not sit on top of each other on the $N$ axis either. The
1.5M rung contributes two different non-embedding parameter counts, 1,567,200
for the canonical run (which counts the $128 \times 160 = 20{,}480$-parameter
position table) and 1,546,720 for the two other-family runs (which do not);
the difference between them is exactly one position table.

\subsection{Why the architecture changed mid-campaign, and why it was invisible}
\label{sec:act4-cause}

The cause of the change is now known, and it is worth stating separately,
because it is a \emph{process} failure and not a modelling one. Git
archaeology settles it. % claims: A4-19

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{src/scaling/} was \textbf{empty before commit \texttt{f1eaf77}}.
      \texttt{src/scaling/ladder.py} is \emph{new} in that commit, and it
      builds \texttt{ALiBiTransformer} with 4 heads --- the \emph{second}
      family's architecture.
\item The \texttt{ASPECT\_LADDER} shape table that commit introduces is
      $\texttt{dim} = 32 \times \texttt{layers}$, that is $128/4$, $160/5$,
      $192/6$, $224/7$, $256/8$ and $288/9$, which matches the shapes of the
      \textbf{canonical MiniGPT} runs exactly.
\item So the committed ladder script encodes the \emph{second} family's
      architecture with the \emph{first} family's shapes, and the script that
      trained the canonical MiniGPT rungs \textbf{was never committed at
      all}.
\end{itemize}

The two families coexist in one ladder because \textbf{nothing recorded which
script produced which points}. The runs that carry an \texttt{architecture}
block are the ones re-run after \texttt{f1eaf77} (or after \texttt{0932d19},
which added the block); the runs without one are the older ones. The ladder's
shape table, the fit, the ledger and the figures all treated the pooled points
as one model family. % claims: A4-01, A4-19

This is the taxonomy's largest entry (Section~\ref{sec:act10b}), and it is
worth naming what it is \emph{not}: it is not a hypothesis about position
encodings that did not pan out, and it is not a bug fix. It is a training
pipeline that was never in the repository, so a mid-campaign change of model
class was invisible to every downstream artefact --- including the ledger that
was supposed to be the record of exactly this. % claims: A10-03

The generalisable lesson is the one this paper's abstract states:
\textbf{a good-looking fit is not evidence of a good fit}. A fit is a
statement about a \emph{set of models}, and a set can be heterogeneous
without anything in the fit complaining: the withdrawn fit's residuals are
still flat in $\log N$ and $\log D$. You need the points to be one model
family, and the only way to know that is to \emph{tag every point with its
configuration and check}.

movement is itself worth reporting. \textbf{(i) Stability}: under total $N$ the

and under non-embedding $N$ they agree to $0.001$ ($+0.407$ against $+0.407$),

were added. \textbf{(ii) Plausibility of $E$}: both fitted values lie below the

both axes instead of preferring one. The axis is therefore chosen by
convention, and both conventions are reported side by side throughout, because
the choice no longer changes the qualitative answer: \emph{both} contain

argued against the corpus's unigram entropy of $4.9946$ nats, which is a
ceiling on a conditional entropy rather than a competitor to it, and it was
then re-made against the model's own achieved loss. On the widened ladder that
check admits both conventions, so it is reported as a \emph{bound that is
respected} rather than as a ground for choosing. What the paper does not claim
is that the two axes agree on the point estimate --- they differ by $0.18$ in
$\alpha$ --- only that widening the ladder removed the reasons for preferring
one axis on fit quality. % claims: A4-11, A4-17

Two checks confirm that the axis is not inflated by an artefact of the
artefacts. The checkpoint file's parameter \emph{sum} double-counts the tied
output embedding --- 3,643,040 against a recorded 2,605,120 at the 1.5M rung,
a difference of exactly $6{,}487 \times 160$ --- but the CSV's $N$ column is
the non-embedding count and is not that sum, so the axis is not inflated by the
double count; and the vocabulary is shared (6,487 at every rung), so any
correction for the embeddings would be a constant, not a per-rung error.

\subsection{What the profile likelihood says, and what it does not}

confidence statement, so the paper does not quote one. Instead it profiles

recorded. The \emph{compatible range} is the set of $\alpha$ values whose fit
comes within 0.001 in $R^2$ of the best fit. On the total-parameter axis it
is $[0.34,\ 0.82]$ and it contains Chinchilla's $0.34$ at its lower end; on
the non-embedding axis the same procedure gives $[0.20,\ 0.60]$, which also

\subsection{The beta profile, and the asymmetry that is the diagnosis}
\label{sec:act4-beta}

The same procedure, with $\beta$ held fixed instead of $\alpha$, gives a
sharper answer, and the difference between the two profiles is the most useful
thing this act has to say. On the total-parameter axis the compatible $\beta$
range is $[0.38,\ 0.54]$, a width of $0.16$ against the $\alpha$ range's
$0.48$; on the non-embedding axis it is also $[0.38,\ 0.54]$, a width of
$0.16$ against $0.40$. \textbf{$\beta$ is three times better constrained than
$\alpha$ on the total-$N$ axis and two and a half times on the non-embedding
axis, on the same 3,463 points.} % claims: A4-18

\textbf{The mechanism, which tells the reader what to do next.} Each run
contributes interior checkpoints at \textbf{fixed $N$ and rising $D$}, so the
within-run $D$ variation constrains $\beta$ tightly --- a single run supplies
hundreds of points at one $N$ and a hundred different $D$ values. $\alpha$,
by contrast, depends on the \textbf{number and spread of distinct $N$ values}
the ladder contains, and the ladder contains eight. So $\alpha$ has little to
work with and $\beta$ has hundreds of points per run. The
consequence for the experimental design is direct: to identify $\alpha$, widen
the range of distinct $N$ values; to identify $\beta$, add $D$. This is the
reason the rescue in Section~\ref{sec:act4-rescue} added a $0.3$M and a
$0.4$M rung rather than more tokens to the rungs it already had.
% claims: A4-15

\textbf{And the asymmetry fixes what the paper may claim about $\beta$.} The
total-$N$ $\beta$ range $[0.38,\ 0.54]$ \textbf{excludes} Chinchilla's
$0.28$, and so does the non-embedding range $[0.38,\ 0.54]$. \emph{No
compatibility claim may be made for $\beta$ at all}, on either axis. A beta
profile that shows the published point outside the range is informative rather
than embarrassing, and it is shown: Figure~\ref{fig:act4-profile}, top right.
% claims: A4-18, A4-12

Two things follow. First, \textbf{the mixture is visible directly as a
collapse in fit quality}: one law on the mixed set reaches only
$R^2 = 0.7087$, while either pure family fits at $R^2 \geq 0.995$. No
statistical test is needed to see a difference that large, and no amount of
reweighting would rescue it. Second, \textbf{the spread across variants is
the measurement that says $\alpha$ is not identified here}: it runs from
$0.057$ to $0.589$ across the defensible fits, and on the paper's own
axis the profile's compatible range spans $[0.34,\ 0.82]$. A ladder of
twenty-three runs over eight rungs cannot pin down an exponent four orders of
magnitude below the published regime, and the statement in
Section~\ref{sec:act4-profile} is a statement about that width rather than a
point result. % claims: A4-08

The methodological lesson generalises past this act, and it is the same lesson
that Act~5c teaches from the other direction. A fit is a statement about a
\emph{set of models}, and a set can be heterogeneous without anything in the
fit complaining. The ladder's homogeneity was an assumption, never a
measurement, and it was wrong in a way a residual plot could not have shown:
the residuals of the mixed fit are still flat in $\log N$ and $\log D$
($-0.0047$ and $-0.0047$), because two different learning curves can be
fitted by one curve without leaving a visible signature in the residual
correlation. What exposed it was a provenance check --- reading the run
records --- not a better statistic, and the cause of the change is now known
too (Section~\ref{sec:act4-cause}). The generalisable form of the lesson is
the one the taxonomy in Section~\ref{sec:act10b} records: \textbf{a
good-looking fit is not evidence of a good fit}. You need the points to be one
model family, and the only way to know that is to tag every point with its
configuration and check.

\subsection{The other family's 129.6--223.8 nats are an initialisation property, not a metric bug}
\label{sec:act4-other}

The other family's first logged validation losses range from 129.57 to 223.77
nats --- the five values are 129.57, 134.70, 181.65, 183.96 and 223.77 ---
which is fifteen to twenty-five times the uniform-over-vocabulary loss of
$\ln 6{,}487 = 8.78$ nats. A number that absurd on its face deserves
the same treatment this project gave defect D18, which returned 11.98 nats for
a model whose real loss is about 2.5. Three candidate causes, and the
measurement. % claims: A4-14

\paragraph{Is the loss summed rather than averaged?} If it were summed over
positions and batch it would be about $8.78 \times 32 \times 128 = 36{,}000$
nats. The observed 129.6 is neither 8.78 nor 36,000, so the metric is an
average. \paragraph{Is the metric misaligned, a D18-style target shift?} The
same code path computes the canonical family's step-1 loss as 8.80, against a
uniform-vocabulary cross-entropy of 8.78; a shift would corrupt both families
equally. The metric is correct. \paragraph{Therefore it is the model.} Building
that model class from the repository's own code, with the repository's own
seed, on the same data, reproduces the effect to within 2\%: 133.25 against
129.57 at the 1.5M rung, 184.49 against 183.96 at 4.1M and 224.99 against
223.77 at 9.0M, while the canonical family reproduces at 8.82 against 8.80.
% claims: A4-14

\textbf{This makes the contamination more serious, not less.} Those five runs
were not broken and not divergent: they were good models that trained normally
and finished at 3.578 nats at the 1.5M rung against the canonical family's
3.057. They were invisible because their loss trajectory started fifteen to
twenty-five times above uniform and ended half a nat worse --- a
plausible-looking curve, at a plausible-looking $N$, with a
plausible-looking $R^2$, and nothing in the artefacts distinguished it from a
canonical run until the provenance was read by hand. The defect was not a
broken run that a reader could have spotted; it was a working run that a fit
could happily describe. % claims: A4-14

\subsection{The rescue: six arms, eight rungs, and a null that is now credible}
\label{sec:act4-rescue}

The 4.1M and 9.0M rungs were entirely of the other family, so the clean fit had
only four rungs and four distinct $N$ values, which was the main reason
$\alpha$ was weakly identified. The rescue is now \textbf{complete}:
\texttt{src/scaling/rescue.py} re-ran six arms with the canonical
architecture, with every logged point tagged by a config hash computed from the
live model at the moment it is logged rather than retroactively from a rung
table, because retroactive tagging is exactly what let the mid-campaign
architecture change hide in plain sight. % claims: A4-15

\textbf{The plan was set by the asymmetry in
Section~\ref{sec:act4-beta}.} $\alpha$'s identifiability rests on the
\textbf{number and spread of distinct $N$ values}, not on $D$: the interior
checkpoints already give each run about 230 points at \emph{fixed $N$ and
rising $D$}, and the $D = 20N$ line makes $N$ and $D$ collinear across
rungs. Adding a point to a line that already holds four $N$ values buys
little; widening the $N$ range buys a lot.

\subsection{The pre-registered falsification condition}
\label{sec:act4-falsification}

The condition was written before any run of the sweep (2026-10-15) and is
reproduced here in the form it was recorded. The claim under test was: \emph{``Loss
scales predictably with $N$ (non-embedding parameters) and $D$ (training
tokens) in the $\sim$0.8M--9M regime ($L(N,D) = E + A/N^\alpha + B/D^\beta$).''}
It would be \textbf{falsified} if either (i) the fitted model failed to beat a
constant-loss baseline on held-out points ($R^2 \leq 0$ against the rung-mean
baseline), or (ii) the residuals showed systematic curvature in $\log N$ or
$\log D$ indicating a missing lower- or higher-order term rather than
uncorrelated noise. Three additional acceptance checks were recorded at the
same time: every rung must produce at least 50 distinct $D$ values, the
embedding share must be reported per rung, and a single-seed rung may not be
presented with an error bar. Anything decided after seeing the fit is
labelled exploratory in this report.

\textbf{The condition was NOT FALSIFIED for the clean fit, inside the regime it
was scoped to.} Every held-out rung between 0.8M and 9.0M is predicted with
$R^2 > 0$ (0.887, 0.964, 0.981, 0.995, 0.992 and 0.988 on the total-parameter
axis), and the residual correlations with $\log N$ and $\log D$ are $-0.00081$
and $-0.0062$ --- negligible curvature. The two rungs the rescue added below
0.8M are a separate matter: holding out 0.3M or 0.4M gives $R^2 = -435$ and
$-71$, because the fit is being extrapolated below the range it was fitted on.
That is a statement about \emph{where the law applies} rather than a
falsification of the claim, and it is reported because the ledger's own
condition makes it visible: the power law holds across the 0.8M--9M decade this
act is about, and stops holding one bin below it. % claims: A4-03

The same protocol applied to the \emph{mixed} set would have falsified it:
the mixed fit's leave-one-rung-out $R^2$ is negative on five of its six rungs
($-61.20$, $-0.34$, $-0.49$, $-0.03$, $-1.55$ and $+0.42$). That is the
condition doing its job in hindsight, and it is the reason the mixed fit's
$R^2 = 0.7087$ is reported as a collapse rather than as a result. The
ledger's earlier bracket $\alpha \in [0.445, 0.552]$, $\beta \in [0.209,
0.290]$ belonged to the withdrawn fit and is not carried forward; what this
paper quotes instead is the profile likelihood's compatible range, with the
fold-wise estimates reported individually beside it and the other parameter
convention reported alongside rather than instead of it. % claims: A4-02

\subsection{Caveats on the $\alpha$ comparison, and the 1.5M rung}
\label{sec:act4-1p5m}
\label{sec:act4-caveats}

\textbf{The tokenizer caveat.} The ladder's BPE has a 6,487-token vocabulary at
2.2726 characters per token, and its base alphabet contains 2,486 characters,
most of them rare Unicode. A byte-fallback scheme would compress better
\emph{and} preserve a 0\% unknown-token rate, which the current scheme does
not: it is a legitimate improvement we did not make. The exponents are
internally consistent with the points they were fitted to, but they are
weakly identified at this scale (see below), and any comparison of our
$\alpha$ with a published value is therefore \textbf{caveated}; we do not
present it as a like-for-like comparison.

\textbf{The entropy caveat.} $E = +1.964$ is an \emph{extrapolated asymptote}
from an eight-rung ladder and not a measured irreducible loss. The corpus's
measured unigram entropy under this vocabulary is $4.9946$ nats on the
held-out region, and the fitted $E$ is $0.39\times$ that, so the parameter
must not be read as the corpus's floor (Section~\ref{sec:act4-entropy}).
% claims: A4-17

\textbf{The schedule caveat.} Interior ladder points are mid-schedule: the
learning rate has not been annealed at the point where the run stopped, so
those points carry an unquantified bias. This is the reason the annealed-endpoint
sensitivity analysis exists, and it applies to the clean fit exactly as it
applied to the withdrawn one.

\textbf{The 1.5M rung is not one configuration, and now we know why.} Its
three recorded runs carry two different non-embedding parameter counts
(1,567,200 and 1,546,720) and final validation losses spanning 3.0468--3.5780,
a spread of 0.53 nats. The reason is not seed noise: two of the three runs
are a different architecture, one with a learned position table and two
without, and the 20,480-parameter difference between the two counts is
exactly one position table. % claims: A4-05
 We therefore present the ``seed spread'' and
``noise floor'' statements of this act as \textbf{exploratory}: the fit is
reported as measured, and the rung heterogeneity is a known heterogeneity in
the input data, resolved for the fit by dropping the other family rather than
by pooling it.

\textbf{The 0.8M rung carried a third architecture, and that was the worst
fold.} Its recorded $N$ of 694,144 matches no known shape, it has no
architecture block, it ran on CPU, and it sat at $D/N = 22.7$. It carried the
act's worst hold-out fold on the four-rung ladder ($R^2 = 0.508$
non-embedding, $0.779$ total), which is why it was the first rung the rescue
replaced: the canonical 0.8M run now in the fit gives $0.810$ and $0.887$, and
the three unidentifiable runs are excluded from every number this act reports
(Section~\ref{sec:act4-naudit}). % claims: A4-20, A4-21

\textbf{Identification.} $\alpha$ is weakly identified at this scale, and the
evidence for that statement is in the act itself: eight distinct $N$ values,
five rungs with a single seed, a profile's compatible range that spans
$[0.34,\ 0.82]$, and a spread across defensible fit variants that runs from
0.057 to 0.589. The uncertainty to quote is the profile's compatible range
and the eight fold-wise estimates, not the point estimate and not the tracked
JSON's covariance errors, which are not credible at a Jacobian condition
number of $1.9\times10^{6}$. $\beta$ is better identified (a compatible range
of width $0.16$ on the total-$N$ axis) but its range \emph{excludes}
Chinchilla's $0.28$, so no compatibility claim may be made for it.
A third, independent check agrees and is worth one sentence: the ladder's
local exponent --- computed from adjacent rungs without the five-parameter fit
at all --- is made flat by $E = 0.20$, where it averages $0.157$ with a spread
of $0.053$, so the check does not reproduce either published exponent either.
% claims: A4-23
% claims: A4-18

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:act4-conclusion}

\textbf{What the measurement supports.} The ladder was two architecture
families, and that is now a measurement rather than a story: 14 canonical runs
and 5 of the other family, separable by a factor of fifteen in first logged
loss and by the presence of a learned position table. The cause of the change
is also known now, and it is a process failure --- a training pipeline that
was never in the repository --- rather than a modelling one
(Section~\ref{sec:act4-cause}). The ladder was also recorded on a parameter
axis the published exponents do not use, and the paper now states which axis
it uses and why; on the widened ladder the two conventions agree on
everything except the value of $\alpha$, so the choice is made on the
published convention (Section~\ref{sec:act4-axis}). On the canonical
family alone, on the total-parameter axis, a Chinchilla-form law fitted to
3,463 interior points over 8 rungs and 8 distinct $N$ values gives
$\alpha = +0.589$, $\beta = +0.461$ and $E = +1.964$ at $R^2 = 0.9960$, with
a leave-one-rung-out $R^2$ of 0.887--0.995 across the 0.8M--9.0M regime. The
pre-registered falsification condition was not falsified for that fit, and the
residuals show no curvature. The statement the act
supports about the published exponents is a \textbf{compatibility-and-
indiscriminability statement}: the $\alpha$ profile's compatible range is
$[0.34,\ 0.82]$, it contains $0.34$ and also $0.50$, and the ladder is
therefore compatible with both published exponents and discriminates between
neither. The rescue is finished, and it made the null credible rather than
overturning it: four more distinct $N$ values and a second $D/N$ ratio moved
the range's width from $0.46$ to $0.48$ without narrowing it. The contamination
is visible directly as a collapse in fit quality: one law on the mixed set
reaches $R^2 = 0.7087$ while either pure family fits at $R^2 \geq 0.995$.
% claims: A4-06, A4-09,
% claims: A4-19

\textbf{What it does not support.} It does not support the withdrawn claim:
$\alpha = 0.445$ describes the other family, not these models, and the
``$R^2 \geq 0.985$ on every rung'' headline was produced by that family
too. It does not support a point claim about Chinchilla: the paper does not
say that our $\alpha$ is $0.34$, nor that our $\alpha$ agrees with $0.34$,
because the compatible range is too wide to say either thing. It does not
support \emph{any} compatibility claim for $\beta$, whose compatible range
$[0.38,\ 0.54]$ excludes the published $0.28$ on both axes. It does not
support a claim that this ladder identifies $\alpha$ at all: across
the defensible fits $\alpha$ runs from 0.057 to 0.589, and the honest result of
the act is that a ladder of twenty-three runs over eight rungs cannot pin down
an exponent four orders of magnitude below the published regime. It does not
support the claim that the two parameter conventions give the same point
estimate, because they do not ($0.589$ against $0.407$). It does not support
reading $E$ as the corpus's irreducible loss: the
measured unigram entropy is $4.9946$ nats and the fitted $E$ lies below it
under both conventions. And it does not support extrapolation: the ladder
spans 0.3M--9.0M non-embedding parameters and 6.2M--180M tokens, and
nothing here licenses a claim about models larger than the ones we trained.
% claims: A4-16, A4-17, A4-18

The negative result has a mechanism, and the mechanism is arithmetic rather
than incidental. With per-token acceptance $\alpha$, one round yields
$(1 - \alpha^{K+1}) / (1 - \alpha)$ tokens for $2K$ forwards. That ratio is
below 1.0 for every $K > 1$ even at $\alpha = 1$, because $2K$ forwards buy at
most $K+1$ tokens: $K > 1$ is a net loss on forward count before any hardware
consideration enters. A real speedup additionally requires the target forward
to be far more expensive than the draft forward. At 1.5M--6.3M
non-embedding parameters neither forward is compute-bound; both are
dispatch-latency-bound, so the target's batching advantage is negligible and
the extra draft forward is pure cost.

Acceptance was \emph{high}, which is what makes this interesting rather than
worse: the median acceptance at $K = 1$ is 0.857 for both gaps, and the ledger
records a first-token acceptance of 0.90 for the 6.3M target against 0.82 for
the 2.6M target over 50 samples --- the \emph{larger} gap accepts more, because
the 6.3M target is the better-trained model (2.5152 against 2.8111), not
because the draft is closer to it in size. All three rungs share corpus,
tokenizer and recipe, which keeps their next-token distributions close. % claims: A5C-02
 The
negative result is therefore not ``speculative decoding fails''; it is that at
this scale the forward-count budget dominates acceptance.

The general lesson, stated so that it outlives our models: speculative
decoding pays when the target forward is compute-bound, so that verifying $K+1$
positions in one pass is materially cheaper than $K+1$ sequential passes. That
is the regime of memory-bandwidth-bound decoding at large scale. Our models are
too small to enter it, so the correct conclusion is about our scale, not about
the method, which is why this is reported as a bounded negative rather than a
refutation.

\subsection{Methodology: the bug that a differential test could not find}
\label{sec:act5-methodology}

\paragraph{The double-fed context.} Speculative decoding re-fed the entire
sequence at the top of every round, on top of an already-prefilled KV cache, in
both the draft loop and the target verification loop. The context was therefore
double-counted, and the target's distribution $p$ differed from the draft's
$q$ even when draft and target were the \emph{same weights}. The symptom was
an acceptance rate of 15--40\% at a zero draft-target gap, where it must be
exactly 100\%.

\paragraph{Why it failed silently.} The cached and the uncached equivalence
tests both passed, because both paths shared the bug and therefore agreed with
each other while both were wrong. \textbf{A test that compares two
implementations cannot detect a fault that both of them share.} What finally
localised the fault in minutes was an absolute sanity invariant that no
differential test would have suggested: acceptance must be exactly 100\% at a
zero draft--target gap, because $p = q$ makes $\min(1, p/q) = 1$ for every
token. The regression test \texttt{test\_zero\_gap\_acceptance\_is\_total}
asserts 100\% acceptance at $K \in \{1,2,3,5,7\}$ for both sampling and greedy.

\paragraph{What it cost.} Before the fix, zero-gap acceptance at $K = 3$ was
15.6\% and throughput was 28.9 tokens/s; after the fix, acceptance is 100\% and
throughput is 309.8 tokens/s, a 10.7$\times$ improvement. \textbf{All 5c numbers
taken before that commit are void.} % claims: A5C-03
 The zero-gap configuration is itself a
negative result and is expected to be one: at zero gap no target forward pass
is saved, while each round still costs $2K$ forwards against $K$ for plain
decoding, so it runs at 0.41--0.50$\times$ plain cached generation. Its
purpose is to prove correctness end-to-end on a real model, not to measure
performance.

\paragraph{The same class of error, in the same act.} The Act~5a ledger
originally asserted that ``greedy/top-$k$/top-$p$/beam all use it [the cache]''.
That was false when written: \texttt{beam\_search} had no cache parameter in
any commit, and \texttt{tests/test\_kv\_cache.py} covered greedy, sampling and
top-$p$ but never tested beam at all. The assertion survived into a report
because nothing behind it could fail. After the fix, beam does use a
per-beam cache and the conclusion held, but the number moved: beam-4 is
4.26$\times$ greedy's time for 0.061 bits, where the uncached measurement had
said 4.9$\times$. Instrumenting forward passes and cache lengths, rather than
re-reading a ledger sentence, is what found it.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:act5-conclusion}

\textbf{What the measurement supports.} A KV cache produces byte-identical
output, converts autoregressive decoding from quadratic to linear in context,
and repays its memory once the sequence exceeds the embedding dimension.
Sharing KV heads buys an 8$\times$ cache reduction and a 12.1\% FLOP
reduction --- contradicting the course material's claim that these techniques do
not reduce compute --- at a small but statistically significant quality cost of
about 0.026--0.029 nats at the 2.6M rung. Speculative decoding is implemented
correctly and is nonetheless a 2--4$\times$ loss at every configuration tested,
for a reason that is arithmetic.

\textbf{What it does not support.} It does not support quoting 18.36$\times$ as
a property of the models in this paper: that is a mechanism microbenchmark on
a purpose-built context-1024 model, and the canonical models have a 128-token
context. It does not support a portable speedup figure for speculative decoding
from CPU best-of-3 timings of 0.03--0.11 s. It does not support a claim about
sliding-window attention at long context, because that sweep was dropped for
cost (a measured 42.1 minutes per run against a 1.8--2.4 hour estimate) and
the surviving measurement is at context 128 only, where $W \geq$ context is a
no-op and only $W = 64$ is non-trivial. % claims: A5B-06
 It does not support treating the
zero-gap total-variation comparison as evidence that the operator is correct
on a real model: at zero gap speculative decoding \emph{is} plain sampling, so
that comparison is near-tautological and proves only that the code runs. % claims: A5C-04

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:act6-conclusion}

\textbf{What the measurement supports.} Across a 15-configuration sweep,
diversity costs quality essentially monotonically (correlation $+0.87$ with
distinct-2). Beam-4 produces more typical text than greedy for 0.061 bits at
4.26$\times$ the time; beam-8 is worse than beam-4 at 2.24$\times$ the cost;
greedy is the quality/cost knee. Best-of-$n$ reranking recovers $+0.272$ bits
but remains 0.259 bits worse than greedy, and the reason is structural.
Self-consistency gives no gain at any vote count, because the argmax it
amplifies is exactly at chance and is systematically biased towards one class.

\textbf{What it does not support.} It does not support any absolute claim
about text quality, because the judge is the model under test. It does not
support a claim that self-consistency is useless in general: it supports the
narrower claim that self-consistency cannot manufacture task competence that
is not there, and it does so by measuring the competence and finding it
absent. It does not support a claim that beam search is the right decoder

\section{Act 7: instruction tuning and parameter-efficient adaptation}
\label{sec:act7}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:act7-setup}

The 6.3M canonical model, a synthetic seven-task family, 512 training and 128
held-out items, three seeds, 24 epochs, teacher-forced exact match with 95\%
Wilson intervals. The held-out split is disjoint from the training split by
construction, and the disjointness is asserted by the harness.

\subsection{7a: the zero-shot floor, and the control that makes it assertable}
\label{sec:act7a}

The base model scores 0.0000 on all 128 held-out items, so every number above
zero in this act is attributable to adaptation. That floor is only worth
stating if the metric could have detected a nonzero score, and a positive
control establishes it: an oracle emitting the correct answer scores 1.0
through the teacher-forced path, through the greedy path and through the exact
ICL scoring path, while the real model scored zero on identical items. The
harness is sound and the 7a floor is assertable. % claims: A7-01

\subsection{7b revisited: the LIMA premise, not the LIMA claim}
\label{sec:act7-lima}

The full fine-tuning curve is 0.0703 $\to$ 0.1667 $\to$ 0.3255 $\to$ 0.5651 at
$n = 16/64/256/512$, with no saturation: it is still rising by 0.24 from 256
to 512. The $n = 16$ row is identical across all three seeds (0.0703 three
times), because one batch per epoch gives the seeded shuffle nothing to
shuffle; that row is effectively $n = 1$ and is annotated as such in the table,
in the JSON and in the figure, and carries no error bar. % claims: A7-07


This is \emph{not} a contradiction of LIMA. LIMA argues that the capability
already exists in the base model and that supervised fine-tuning merely
elicits it. Here both zero-shot and in-context learning are 0.0000, so there
is no latent instruction-following to elicit: the curve is \emph{learning a
skill}, not aligning one. The supported claim is that LIMA's ``less is more''
finding is not supported at 6.3M, together with the mechanism sentence above. % claims: A7-08


One negative inside the positive curve is retained rather than smoothed away.
The \texttt{add} task scores 0.9452 on \emph{train} items (69 of 73) and
\textbf{0.0000 on held-out items} (0 of 18). The held-out guesses are off by
one --- $8+2 \to 11$, $7+7 \to 15$, $6+8 \to 15$, $1+8 \to 10$ --- which is the
signature of memorising seen sums rather than learning addition. It is a
result, not a failure of the harness. % claims: A7-09

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:act7-conclusion}

\textbf{What the measurement supports.} The zero-shot floor is 0.0000 and is
assertable, because a positive control confirms the metric registers 1.0 when
the answer is present. Supervised fine-tuning scales monotonically with the
instruction-set size, with no saturation in range, reaching 0.5651 at 512
examples. That figure carries two separate spreads and they should not be
confused. Across the three seeds the runs are
$[0.5938,\ 0.5234,\ 0.5781]$ --- s.d. $0.0370$, range $0.0704$ --- and
those seeds vary optimisation \emph{order} only, because the base weights are
pinned, so between-initialisation variance is unmeasured. Separately, re-running
the identical recipe on a different device returned $0.6016$, which is $0.0078$
above the best of the three seeds ($0.21$ s.d.) and so just outside their
range; because it is \emph{the same seed} on different hardware it is not a
fourth independent draw but a device-level reproducibility band of about
$0.008$ on a fixed seed. % claims: A7-10
At a matched 20,000-parameter budget, adapters score 0.2136, LoRA
0.1823, prompt tuning 0.0859 and prefix tuning 0.0000 --- and prefix tuning's
zero is a failure to fit, not a scoring artefact. LoRA rank is a smooth
capacity dial across $r \in \{1,\dots,64\}$, reaching 0.5078 at 11.5\% of full
fine-tuning's trainable parameters. In-context learning is not absent: the
model copies the demonstration's answer as its first token on 18/18 same-task
items.

\textbf{What it does not support.} It does not support a claim that in-context
learning is absent at 6.3M. It does not support the ordering of adapters above
LoRA. It does not support a claim about natural instructions: the task family
is synthetic. It does not support a claim that the $n = 16$ row is an
$n = 3$ result. And the error bars throughout are within-initialisation
optimisation-order variance, because the base weights are pinned, so
between-initialisation variance is unmeasured.

\section{Act 8: grounding, hallucination, and tool use}
\label{sec:act8}

\subsection{8a: one prompt builder, and a context budget denominated in tokens}
\label{sec:act8a}

Two defects in the v1 retrieval path are fixed here, and the cost of one of
them is worth a number. The v1 code passed \texttt{context\_length // 2} as a
limit in \emph{characters}; on the canonical 128-token window that admitted 64
characters, about 32 tokens, of a 302-token chunk --- so 9.2\% of the
characters, and 10.6\% of the tokens, of the retrieved context survived. The
token-based budget admits 77 tokens instead of 32, a factor of 2.4 more, and
the assembled prompt measures 120 tokens, within the 128-token window.
\texttt{fit\_context()} binary-searches the longest context prefix whose
\emph{assembled} prompt fits in tokens while holding back an answer reserve,
and then re-encodes to verify the result rather than trusting the arithmetic;
it raises rather than returning a prompt that cannot be answered in. % claims: A8A-01
 The second
defect was that retrieval and supervised fine-tuning used different prompt
strings, so a model tuned on the instruction contract was evaluated on a
different one; one formatter now serves both. % claims: A8A-02

prompt moves the answer; the second is the measure the test is powered for.

swapped.

correct. \emph{Below chance} is not: 9 of 40 against a per-item chance of
0.1838 is not significantly below chance, and the closed-book point estimate

\subsection{The retrieved condition, and the retriever that was broken}
\label{sec:act8-retrieval}

The first version of the retrieved condition queried the retriever with the
instruction text, whose terms (\texttt{what}, \texttt{number}, \texttt{does},
\texttt{the}, \texttt{passage}, \texttt{state}) cannot come from a passage about
a number. Term-overlap ranking degenerated to a constant and the tie-break
returned the same chunk for every item, so the earlier 0/40 was a
\emph{query-design failure}, not a retriever failure and emphatically not a
finding. The diagnosis was performed before the claim was revised, and the
query is now the item's \emph{lexical anchor}: content words from a different
sentence of the same document, excluding the number, so the retriever has a
topical handle but never sees the number or the answer-bearing sentence. The
condition is therefore answer-agnostic rather than a disguised oracle.

The template evidence is at the
token level: \texttt{CALC(} tokenises to $[49, 4083, 49, 22]$, that is
\texttt{['C', 'AL', 'C', '(']}, and after that prefix the next-token

(47.5\% against 2.5\%) is reported rather than smoothed over: the echo is a
mode phenomenon that $T = 1.0$ sampling dilutes.

says so explicitly: \textbf{the format is acquired and the operation is
absent}. The model's own ``expression'' template, echoed back to it, is enough
to produce a tool call it cannot complete.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:act8-conclusion}

\textbf{What the measurement supports.} At 6.3M, supplying a passage does not
measurably change what the model says. The model identifies which number a
passage asserts at chance (9/40 under oracle against 11/40 closed-book, McNemar
$p = 0.625$), it follows a counterfactual edit in 0 of 40 items, and a
topic-matched passage containing no number scores exactly the same 9/40 as the
asserting passage. The mechanism is a generic context-number bias: a passage
raises the likelihood of the numbers in it by 0.6--0.8 nats whether or not it
asserts them, and the only content-specific residual is $+0.204$ nats at
$p = 0.030$, which comes with no behavioural change. Model-initiated tool use
is 0/40 well-formed calls, with 19/40 greedy generations echoing the
template and the argument \texttt{s} in all of them.

\textbf{What it does not support.} It does not support a claim that the model
is \emph{below} chance: the interval contains the chance level and the
closed-book point estimate is higher. It does not support any comparison
between conditions on the absolute hallucination rate, which is a ceiling of
1.000 everywhere. % claims: A8C-05
 It does not support a claim about factual truth: numbers proxy
for atomic claims and there is no fact base, so this measures
\emph{groundedness} against a supplied source, not truth. It does not support a
generalisation about retrieval-augmented generation at any other scale.

\section{Act 9: interpretability}
\label{sec:act9}

This act asks where the model stores what it fails to use. It uses the pinned
canonical 6.3M model --- 8 layers, 8 heads, 64 heads in total, dimension 256,
context 128 --- and a held-out slice of 64 sequences of 128 tokens from the C4
shard at document offset 300,000, far beyond anything the training sweep
touched.

\textbf{The intact baseline is measured in this run}, on that slice:
cross-entropy 2.8551 nats, perplexity 17.376. Every delta in this section is
therefore \emph{internal} to the experiment and is \textbf{not} comparable to
the published 2.5152 validation loss of the same checkpoint on the full
validation set. % claims: A9-01

\textbf{This act was measured twice, and the second measurement changed one of
its claims.} The first version of 9c reported that the model encodes which
number a passage asserts and then destroys it before the output. A control set
was then run --- five conditions for the lens, a chance anchor for the
attribution method, and three checks on the head claim --- and the controls
went \emph{against} that version of 9c. The strong claim is withdrawn below,
in the form the controls support, and the withdrawal is part of the result
rather than a footnote to it.

\subsection{9a: causal head ablation with a random-ablation null}
\label{sec:act9a}

Every head is ablated in turn --- its attention output is zeroed before it is
mixed back into the residual stream --- and the held-out loss is re-measured.
The essential control is a \emph{random-ablation null}: the same number of
heads removed at random, with the same budget, so that a large $\Delta$ can be
distinguished from the fact that some heads matter more than others by
position alone. Without that control the experiment would measure the head
\emph{index}, not the head. The null is 40 random single-head sets, with
random 2-, 3- and 4-head sets for the combinations.

\textbf{The finding of this subsection is a redundancy, and it comes first
because it changes what the rest of the section means.} Ablating layer~0
head~5 together with layer~0 head~2 costs only $+0.0234$ nats --- which is
\emph{exactly} what ablating layer~0 head~2 alone costs --- so the two heads
are redundant (redundancy $0.97$), and a redundant partner is silently
covering for the head whose removal is most visible in isolation. The
converse pair shows that this is not general fragility: ablating layer~0
head~5 together with layer~1 head~6 costs $+0.9591$ nats against a sum of
singles of $+0.9079$, which is additive, so the effect is specific to the
redundant partner rather than a property of removing heads at all. The
reading is that the effect is carried by a \emph{group} of layer-0 heads, and
that a single ablated head appears to carry the model only because a
redundant partner is covering for it. The full control set that establishes
this is Table~\ref{tab:act9a-controls} below.

attribute to resampling noise --- and that is the claim the paper makes. The

\subsection{9c: the logit lens, and the control that decided it}
\label{sec:act9c}

The lens is the model's \emph{own} linear readout --- the final norm plus the
tied unembedding --- applied to each layer's residual at the answer position,
so that ``decodable'' means decodable by a readout the model already owns and
no probe is smuggled in. The measurement is the median rank of the target's
token in that distribution. The items are Act~8's passage items --- 40 of them
in the control run, and the 34 with a located answer token in the first run
--- and not the held-out slice that the head ablation of
Section~\ref{sec:act9a} uses.

The first run measured the median rank of the \emph{asserted} number over 34
items and found it most decodable at layer 5 (median rank 87 of 6,487, the
top 1.3\% of the vocabulary), rising again at layers 6 and 7, with the number
never the lens argmax at any layer on any of the 34 items. % claims: A9-05
That is a measurement of a number that \emph{is} in the passage. It is not, on
its own, a measurement of the model reading the passage, and the controls were
run to find out which of the two it was.

\textbf{The control decides it, and it goes against the strong claim.} The
counterfactual-EDITED number reaches \emph{the same} layer-5 median rank as
the asserted number, 82 against 82, and the non-numeric control reaches 86,
which is indistinguishable from it on a 40-item median. A number that appears
in no passage is much worse, at 484, and a random token id much worse still,
at 735. The lens is therefore \textbf{number-specific} --- it is reading the
model's representation of a number that is in the context --- and it is
\textbf{not assertion-specific}: it does not distinguish which number the
passage asserts. % claims: A9-07

\textbf{The claim, in the form the controls support: context numbers are
represented in the residual stream, and assertion-specific content is not.}
The layer-5 spike is therefore evidence for the Act~8 bag-of-numbers bias
(Section~\ref{sec:act8c}), not for assertion-reading. The strong version of
this act's original claim --- that the model encodes \emph{which} number is
asserted and then destroys it before the output --- is \textbf{withdrawn}.

Two runs, two item sets, one picture. The first lens run (34 items with a
located answer token) put the asserted number at median rank 87 at layer 5;
the control run (40 items) puts it at 82. The difference between the two is
the item set, not the measurement.

\textbf{9b and 9c together are one finding.} A linear readout of the
residual stream beats attention-based attribution: the readout places a
context number in the top 1.3\% of the vocabulary at layer 5, while the
attribution never identifies the answer token and puts no more mass on it than
uniform. And even the readout only sees a generic number. Read together, they
say that this stack holds a generic number representation, that attention
says nothing about it, and that the representation does not reach the output.

\subsection{The link to Acts 7b and 8d}
\label{sec:act9-link}

This is the act's reason for existing, and we state it for what it is: a link
between two measurements, and an interpretation of them together rather than a
third statistic. The model holds a \textbf{generic number representation} in
the residual stream and does not carry it to the output. So the model is not
failing to \emph{see} a number --- it is failing to \emph{carry} it --- and
that is consistent with the answer-copying measurement of
Section~\ref{sec:act8b} putting the model at chance, with free generation
producing the right number 0 of 40 times, and with the tool call in
Section~\ref{sec:act8d} being a template echo with the argument \texttt{s}.
% claims: A9-06
The head ablation is compatible with the same picture from the other end: a
group of layer-0 heads carries a disproportionate share of the computation,
and the head whose removal is most visible in isolation is a sharp one.

What the link does \emph{not} say is that the lens has shown the model
reading an assertion. The controls in Section~\ref{sec:act9c} say the
opposite about that, and the link is stated in the form the controls leave
open.

Three boundaries travel with that reading, and they are the boundaries this
act does not claim. First, the lens ranks the number's \emph{first} token, so
a multi-token number is only partially represented by that single token.
Second, head ablation removes a head's \emph{contribution}, not the head: the
head still computes and its value vector still participates in the output
projection, which is the standard causal intervention and an approximation of
``delete the head''. Third, the rollout result is one attribution method on one
prompt family --- the number-assertion family that Act~8 supplies --- and it
is not a general claim about attention interpretability.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:act9-conclusion}

\textbf{What the measurement supports.} On the canonical 6.3M model, the effect
is carried by a \emph{group} of layer-0 heads rather than by one head, and
the redundancy control is the result: ablating L0 H5 together with L0 H2
costs $+0.0234$ nats, the same as L0 H2 alone (redundancy 0.97), while L0 H5
with L1 H6 is additive ($+0.9591$ against a sum of $+0.908$), so the
redundancy is specific and not general fragility. In isolation, exactly one
of 64 heads is causally important by the random-ablation null: layer 0,
head 5, with $\Delta$CE of $+0.832$ nats, a $z$ of $+7.82$, and a $z$ of
$+42.8$ against the null; 59 of the 64 heads have $z \leq 0$, and the same
head is the top head on a second held-out slice ($z = +37.1$). The top-4
combination costs $+1.047$ nats against $+0.071$ for four random heads, and
the interval that travels with it, $[0.0365, 0.6390]$, is a bootstrap over
the four individual deltas rather than over the combination. That head is
sharp: its attention entropy is 0.379 nats against 2.480 nats for the 59
inert heads; it is not an attention sink; and ablating it degrades the model
(CE 2.8551 to 3.6869) rather than collapsing it (uniform is 8.7776).
Against a behavioural ground truth, neither attention rollout nor raw
last-layer attention ever identifies the answer token (0/34 each, against a
uniform chance of 0.0078), and neither puts more mass on it than uniform. And
the logit lens shows a context number decoded at median rank 82 at layer 5 of
6,487 and not carried to the output --- with the counterfactual-EDITED number
reaching the same rank, which is why the claim is about a generic number
representation and not about assertion-reading.

\textbf{What it does not support.} It does not support the claim that the
model encodes which number is asserted: that claim is withdrawn, because the
controls show the lens cannot tell the asserted number from the edited one, or
from a passage that contains no number at all. It does not support a claim
that one head of 64 carries the model, because a backup head covers for L0 H5.
It does not support comparing these deltas with the published validation
loss, because the intact baseline was measured on this 64-sequence slice in
this run. It does not support a general claim about attention interpretability
from one method on one prompt family. And it does not support a claim that the
9b null is a statement about all attribution methods: a heatmap-based reading
of the same heads would have suggested the opposite, which is precisely why we
report the behavioural test rather than the picture.

\textbf{The largest entry is the CAUSE of the Act 4 contamination, and it
carries the project's generalisable lesson, so it is worth stating outside the
table.} \textbf{A good-looking fit is not evidence of a good fit.} The cause is
now known, and it is a \emph{process} failure rather than a modelling one:
\texttt{src/scaling/} was empty before commit \texttt{f1eaf77}, the ladder
script committed in that commit builds the second family's architecture
(ALiBi, 4 heads) with the first family's shapes, and the script that trained
the canonical MiniGPT rungs \emph{was never committed at all}. A training
pipeline that was never in the repository made a mid-campaign change of model
class invisible to every downstream artefact, including the ledger that was
supposed to be the record of exactly this
(Section~\ref{sec:act4-cause}). % claims: A4-19

The contamination itself is the second entry, and it carries the same lesson
in its statistical form. The withdrawn fit had $R^2 = 0.9950$ in sample, flat
residuals in $\log N$ and $\log D$, and a leave-one-rung-out headline of
$R^2 \geq 0.985$ on every held-out rung. It was, in every visible respect, a
good fit --- of the wrong models. It was fitted to 2,776 interior points of
which 51.4\% came from a different architecture family, and that family
occupied the entire top of the ladder. Three things caught it, and the first
is the one that generalises. \textbf{An absolute invariant}: the fit's own
parameters score $R^2 = -1.3123$ on the canonical models' own points, and no
fit of these models can score a negative $R^2$ on their own points. That is
a violation of an absolute, not a comparison between two implementations, and
it is visible without any of the rest of the apparatus. \textbf{A provenance
read}: the run records, the surviving checkpoints' state dicts and the
architecture block that the training code writes into every run's
\texttt{metrics.json} identified \emph{which} runs were contaminated, and the
repair --- a config hash computed from the live model at the moment a point
is logged, rather than retroactively from a rung table --- exists because
retroactive tagging is exactly what let a mid-campaign architecture change
hide in plain sight. \textbf{A reproduction from the repository's own code}:
the other family's 129.57--223.77 nat step-1 losses are an initialisation
property of that model class, not a metric bug and not divergence, because
building that class with the repository's own seed on the same data
reproduces 133.25 / 184.49 / 224.99 against the recorded 129.57 / 183.96 /
223.77, the metric is an average rather than a sum, and it is not misaligned.
Those five runs were good models that trained normally and finished at 3.578
nats against the canonical 3.057, and they were invisible because their
trajectory started fifteen to twenty-five times above uniform. The defect was
not a broken run a reader could have spotted; it was a working run that a fit
could happily describe. % claims: A4-14, A10-03

\textbf{The generalisable form of the lesson is a procedure, not an
admonition.} A fit is a statement about a \emph{set of models}, and a set can
be heterogeneous without anything in the fit complaining. You need the points
to be one model family, and the only way to know that is to tag every point
with its configuration and check. This act also carries the second, smaller
instance of the same family of error: the ladder's $N$ axis was a
non-embedding count while the published exponents use total parameters, an
unstated confound that a comparison with Chinchilla would have carried
straight through. It was caught by the published convention, and by a
plausibility bound that both conventions now respect but neither
establishes: $E$ is $1.964$ and $1.911$ nats on the two axes, both below the
$2.5152$-nat loss a real model of this ladder actually reaches. A third check
was \textbf{withdrawn}: the absolute plausibility comparison on the fitted
irreducible loss was aimed at the corpus's unigram entropy ($4.9946$ nats),
which is a ceiling on a conditional entropy rather than a competitor to it.
The stability check also stopped discriminating: on four rungs the point
estimate and the fold mean agreed on the total axis and disagreed on the
non-embedding axis, and on eight rungs they agree on both. The confound was
not cosmetic: the two axes still give different point estimates ($0.589$
against $0.407$), even though widening the ladder made them agree on the
qualitative verdict. % claims: A4-11, A4-17

The 5c double feed is the cleanest case, because it shows the negative space
around the principle. The cached and the uncached equivalence tests both
\emph{passed}, because both paths shared the bug and therefore agreed with each
other while both were wrong. \textbf{A test that compares two implementations
cannot detect a fault that both of them share.} What localised the fault in
minutes was an absolute invariant that no differential test would have
suggested: acceptance must be exactly 100\% at a zero draft--target gap,
because $p = q$ makes $\min(1, p/q) = 1$ for every token.

The other thirteen have the same shape in different guises. D18, the held-out
metric that compared logits to the wrong position, returned a cross-entropy of
11.98 nats for a model whose real loss is about 2.5 --- absurd on its face,
because uniform over the 6,487-token vocabulary is $\ln 6487 = 8.78$ nats.
D11, where the input text never reached the prompt, was caught by a
per-position accuracy profile: the first response token was unguessable
(0.070) while positions 1--2 were trivially copyable from the true prefix
(0.927 / 0.900), which is diagnostic of a missing input rather than of a weak
model. The truncated 7b demonstrations were caught by a positive control that
recorded the visible-shot count (``2/4 (truncated away)''). The flat
``chance = 1/3'' baseline was caught by counting the candidates per item,
which gives 0.1838. The retriever was caught by looking at what it actually
returned: one distinct document for all 40 items, and the identical top chunk
for two different failing items. The ALiBi arm was caught by a difference of
$4.8\times10^{-8}$ nats, which is float32 round-off. The two mislabelled
sweep sizes were caught by counting the runs in the tracked result files. The
three $N$ conventions in the ladder's own table were caught by an exact
integer audit against three independent sources, which is what identified the
0.8M rung as a third, unidentifiable architecture. And the Act~4 contamination
was caught by a provenance read of the run records plus an absolute sign check
on the fit's own parameters: they score $R^2 = -1.3123$ on the canonical
models' own points, which no fit of these models can do, while a residual plot
stays flat in $\log N$ and $\log D$ and shows nothing at all.

\textbf{One of the fifteen needed a third kind of check as well.} The Act~4
contamination was exposed by an absolute invariant --- a fit of these models
cannot score a negative $R^2$ on their own points --- but identifying
\emph{which} runs were contaminated needed a provenance read of the run
records, the checkpoints' state dicts and the architecture block that the
training code writes into every run's metrics, and explaining \emph{why} the
two families coexist in one ladder needed a third kind of read still: a
repository one, which found that the training script for one of the two
families was never committed. A set of models can be heterogeneous without
any statistic in the fit complaining, and the honest form of the principle is
therefore the one stated above: the invariant exposes the defect, the
provenance read explains it, and the repository read explains the cause.

\textbf{The model of a good control is Act~8's non-numeric passage.} % claims: A10-04
A different sentence of the same document, containing no number, topic- and
length-matched to the oracle passage, scores exactly the same 9/40 as the
oracle passage does. That control did not merely fail to move the
measurement; it moved the \emph{claim}, from ``the model identifies the number
the passage asserts'' to ``the model has a context-number bias''. It is the
one entry in the taxonomy that is a control on a claim rather than a check on
a measurement, and it is the one that changed a section of this paper.

\textbf{What the taxonomy does not say.} It is a taxonomy of the failures
that were \emph{recorded}, in a project whose ledger exists to record them.
It says nothing about failures that were never noticed, it does not claim
that the invariants listed would catch a defect of a kind that none of them
addresses, and it is not a claim about how often such defects occur: these
are the ones this project found. One of the fifteen is not a measurement
failure at all in the strict sense --- the mislabelled sweep sizes
invalidated no measurement, only a description of the sample --- and it is
listed anyway, because the first thing a reader checks about a sweep is how
big it is.

\subsection{10c: ethics metrics}
\label{sec:act10c}

Three instruments were built, run, and read at the floor. The result of this
act is the floor \emph{together with the instrument that produced it and the
detection threshold at which an effect could have been found}. No significance
is manufactured from a model this small. Source:
\texttt{results/v2/act10/ethics.json}.

\paragraph{A stereotype-association probe, with a negative control.} The
statistic is the within-occupation preference for the feminine-associated
attribute set over the masculine-associated one, measured over ten
occupations with a documented association in English and ten negative-control
occupations with none. All three rates are $1.0$: the associated family's
first occupation $10/10$, its second $10/10$, and the negative control
$10/10$. The probe therefore detects a \emph{global attribute preference}
rather than an association --- the model prefers \emph{caring}, \emph{gentle}
and \emph{empathetic} after \emph{every} occupation. The negative control is
what makes that reading possible: without it, a $10/10$ result would have read
as a stereotype finding. The instrument is also \emph{underpowered by
construction}: at $n = 10$ per family only a rate above $0.8099$ is
distinguishable from chance, so the null is as much a statement about the
instrument's resolution as about the model. % claims: A10C-01

applied to the tracked SFT checkpoint

The second slice changes the claim, and it is the more interesting half. On
generic text the tuned model loses 10.7 points of next-token accuracy
(difference $-0.1068$, 95\% $[-0.1284,\ -0.0852]$): a real cost off-target.
But on the instruction responses themselves the accuracy difference is
$-0.0009$ (95\% $[-0.0160,\ +0.0142]$), \emph{not significant} --- so tuning
did \emph{not} make the model worse at the very distribution it was tuned on.
What it gained there is confidence: mean confidence rose from $0.3969$ to
$0.6261$ with the accuracy flat, and the calibration error on that slice rose
from $0.1118$ to $0.2772$.

So the accurate statement is not that tuning traded general competence for
instruction following, but that tuning \textbf{inflated the model's confidence
on its target and traded away accuracy off-target}. The instruction-following
gain ($0.0000 \to 0.6016$) is bought with confidence inflation and a narrower
distribution, not with better prediction. That is also the mechanism behind
Act~7b and Act~8b: the failures there are failures of a \emph{retargeted}
competence, not of a competence that was never there.

The slices are disjoint from the SFT training data at the level that matters:
no held-out example appears in the training split (0 of 128) and no held-out
addition operand pair appears in it (0 of 18, against 73 distinct training
pairs). The 32 response words the two sets share are the shared word pool the
copy tasks draw on by design, which is a property of the task family and not
leakage. % claims: A10C-04

\paragraph{A toxicity lexicon over the model's own generations.} Twelve terms,
scored over the model's own continuations of C4 passages: \textbf{0 of 40},
Wilson 95\% $[0,\ 0.0876]$, against a corpus base rate of $0.0$. Floor.
% claims: A10C-02

\paragraph{Calibration, and a positive result.} Over $4{,}064$ next-token
predictions, accuracy is $0.5295$ at mean confidence $0.5255$, with an
\emph{expected calibration error of $0.0233$}; the bins track closely
(confidence $0.979 \rightarrow$ accuracy $0.962$; confidence
$0.054 \rightarrow$ accuracy $0.074$). This is worth stating plainly, because it
reframes everything else in this paper: the model is a competent,
well-calibrated next-token predictor and an incompetent instruction follower,
grounder and tool user. Every downstream failure reported here is a failure to
\emph{use} the competence, not a failure to have it. % claims: A10C-03

\paragraph{Instruction tuning, and the part of the comparison that is now
measured.} A tracked supervised-fine-tuned checkpoint now exists.
\texttt{src/eval/act10\_sft\_checkpoint.py} re-runs Act~7's full fine-tune

canonical checkpoints' exact format, and its tracked summary is
\texttt{results/v2/act10/sft\_checkpoint.json}. Instruction following
\emph{does} change under instruction tuning: the SFT model's held-out
exact-match accuracy on the 128 held-out Act~7 items is $0.6016$ (77 of 128,

The per-task breakdown is what makes that number worth reading rather than
quoting. The five easy string tasks score $0.842$, $1.0$, $0.944$, $0.611$
and $0.632$, the reverse-order task scores $0.167$, and \textbf{18 of the 24
tasks score exactly $0.0000$} --- every one of them an addition task.
Instruction tuning teaches the \emph{format} and the easy string operations
here; it does not teach addition, and the base model's addition failure
survives fine-tuning unchanged. This reproduces Act~7's own seed-42 figure
($0.5938$) to within one item, on a different device, so it is a
reproduction of a known result rather than a new one. % claims: A10C-05

\paragraph{The calibration half of the comparison is not yet measured.} The
question this section was written to answer is sharper than the one the
artefact can currently answer: \emph{does instruction tuning change
\emph{calibration}, or leave a well-calibrated model that still cannot follow
instructions?} The tracked JSON carries the fine-tune and its held-out
instruction accuracy, but it does \emph{not} carry a calibration measurement
for the SFT model, so the SFT-versus-base calibration comparison is \emph{not}
reported here --- the artefact for it does not exist yet, and producing it
would be a re-measurement, which this revision does not do. What can be said
is narrower and still worth saying: the base model is well calibrated (ECE
$0.0233$) and cannot follow instructions at all ($0.0000$), and after
fine-tuning it follows instructions on $60.16\%$ of the held-out items.
Whether the tuned model is still well calibrated is the open measurement, and
it is recorded as an open item rather than guessed at. % claims: A10C-04

\section{Threats to validity and known limitations}

\subsection{Single-seed sections}
\label{sec:threats-n1}

\textbf{Acts 2 and 3 are single-seed ($n = 1$).} They rank configurations;
they do not establish effects. There are no error bars in those sections, no
significance tests, and no comparative adjectives without ``suggests''. This
is the single most important caveat in the paper, and it is repeated in the
captions of Table~\ref{tab:act2}, Table~\ref{tab:act3} and

\subsection{Instrument and scale caveats, by act}
\label{sec:threats-instrument}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Act 4.} This is the act with the most caveats, and they are
      not all caveats of degree. The previously reported $\alpha = 0.445$ is
      \textbf{withdrawn}: it describes the other architecture family
      ($R^2 = 0.9957$ on that family's points, $-1.312$ on the canonical
      models' own points), and with it the leave-one-rung-out ``$R^2 \geq
      0.985$ on every rung'' headline, which that family produced because it
      occupied the entire top of the ladder. The \emph{cause} of the
      contamination is now known as well, and it is a process failure rather
      than a modelling one: a training pipeline that was never in the
      repository made a mid-campaign change of model class invisible to every
      downstream artefact, including the ledger
      (Section~\ref{sec:act4-cause}). The refit is \textbf{not a minor
      correction}: three things were invalidated by it, including the
      comparison with Chinchilla, and the comparison had to be re-established
      on the surviving fit rather than assumed. The comparison that survives
      is stronger and more negative than a point comparison: the profile
      likelihood's compatible $\alpha$ range is $[0.34,\ 0.82]$, which
      contains $0.34$ and also $0.50$, so this ladder is \textbf{compatible
      with both published exponents and discriminates between neither}, and
      the range's width is the finding. The
      $\beta$ profile is the mirror image: a compatible range of
      $[0.38,\ 0.54]$ that \textbf{excludes} Chinchilla's $0.28$, so no
      compatibility claim may be made for $\beta$ at all. The clean fit is
      itself exploratory: both the family separation and the N-axis
      convention were decided after the provenance was read, five rungs carry a
      single seed, and across the defensible fits $\alpha$ runs from 0.057 to
      0.589, so this ladder does not
      identify $\alpha$ at this scale. The N-axis convention is a decision
      (total parameters) and it is stated rather than implied, but it now rests
      on one ground, the published convention: the physical plausibility bound
      on the fitted irreducible loss is satisfied by both axes ($1.964$ and
      $1.911$ nats, both below the $2.5152$-nat loss a real model reaches) and
      therefore does not discriminate, and the stability check stopped
      discriminating when the ladder was widened. The two axes do not give the
      same point estimate.
      The tracked fit's exact 2,776-point set cannot be recovered, so the
      contamination is established by parameter reproduction rather than by
      re-running the original fit. The per-rung $N$ audit finds
      \textbf{three $N$ conventions in one table, none of them flagged}, and
      the 0.8M rung's recorded $N$ of 694,144 matches no known architecture
      at all: it is a third, unidentifiable model, carrying the act's worst
      hold-out fold, and it was replaced by a canonical re-run
      (Section~\ref{sec:act4-naudit}). Any comparison
      of our exponents with a published value is additionally caveated by the
      tokenizer: a 6,487-token BPE at 2.2726 characters per token whose base
      alphabet is mostly rare Unicode, where byte fallback would compress
      better and preserve a 0\% unknown-token rate. Interior ladder points
      are mid-schedule with the learning rate not annealed, an unquantified
      bias. The $D = 2$M inversion is a null (seed spread 0.186 against a gap
      of 0.057) in a table whose three canonical-rung columns do not match
      the current ladder's logged points (Section~\ref{sec:act4-equalD}). The
      1.5M rung is internally heterogeneous --- it is two architectures, with
      a 0.53-nat final-loss spread across its three recorded seeds
      (Section~\ref{sec:act4-1p5m}) --- which makes the act's seed-spread and
      noise-floor statements exploratory. And the rescue is \textbf{complete}
      (Section~\ref{sec:act4-rescue}): it widened the ladder from four rungs
      and 2,292 points to eight rungs and 3,463 points, moved $\alpha$ from
      $+0.209$ to $+0.589$, and left the qualitative verdict unchanged ---
      the pre-registered gate asked whether the $\alpha$ range would narrow
      materially from $[0.08,\ 0.54]$, and it did not.
\item \textbf{Act 5a.} The 18.36$\times$ speedup was measured on a
      purpose-built context-1024 model; the canonical models have a 128-token
      context, and the number is a microbenchmark of the mechanism.
\item \textbf{Act 5b.} Seed 42's $G = 1$ arm was re-established by inference
      from load semantics and value plausibility, not by re-verification of the
      artifact, which no longer exists; the $n = 5$ analysis is therefore
      primary and the $n = 6$ analysis is the sensitivity check.
\item \textbf{Act 5c.} The timings are CPU best-of-3 at 0.03--0.11 s, a
      consistent comparison within one run and not a portable speedup figure.
      The distribution test is single-prompt, single-token on toy models, plus
      the residual-correction argument. The zero-gap total-variation comparison
      is near-tautological --- at zero gap speculative decoding \emph{is} plain
      sampling --- and so proves that the code runs, not that the operator is
      correct on a real model.
\item \textbf{Act 5, the $G$ curve.} The 2.349 / 2.354 / 2.363 / 2.346 values
      come from the v1 path (500 steps, character tokenizer, single seed) and
      are context only.
\item \textbf{Act 6.} The BPC axis is self-preferring: the judge is the model
      under test. It is valid for ranking configurations against one another
      under a fixed judge and is not an absolute measure of text quality. The
      sweep has no seed-level error bar, because configurations were not
      re-run with a fresh seed set.
\item \textbf{Act 7.} The $n = 16$ row is effectively $n = 1$; the seeds vary
      optimisation order only, so between-initialisation variance is
      unmeasured; the task family is synthetic rather than natural
      instructions; ICL is teacher-forced and so is not comparable to
      free-running numbers elsewhere; and ICL shot selection is cost-driven, so
      only 18 items constitute a clean same-task test.
\item \textbf{Act 8.} The absolute hallucination rate is a ceiling of 1.000 in
      every condition and is never used to rank conditions; 10 of 120
      candidates were dropped for number truncation, which is why the chance
      baseline is per item and equals 0.1838 rather than $1/3$; numbers proxy
      for atomic claims, so the act measures groundedness and not truth; and
      the retrieval anchor is derived from the target document, so the document
      hit rate of 0.975 is optimistic relative to a user's paraphrase.
\item \textbf{Act 9.} The intact baseline was measured on this
      64-sequence held-out slice in this run (CE 2.8551, perplexity 17.376),
      so every ablation delta is internal to the experiment and is not
      comparable to the published 2.5152 validation loss. Head ablation
      removes a head's \emph{contribution}, not the head. The rollout result
      is one attribution method on one prompt family, and the logit lens ranks
      the number's first token, so a multi-token number is only partially
      represented. The logit lens is number-specific and \emph{not}
      assertion-specific, so the strong version of the 9c claim --- that the
      model encodes which number is asserted --- is withdrawn, and the layer-5
      spike is evidence for the Act~8 bag-of-numbers bias rather than for
      assertion-reading. The bootstrap interval that travels with the top-4
      ablation, $[0.0365, 0.6390]$, is over the four individual head deltas
      and does not contain the $+1.047$ combined effect, so that effect is a
      point estimate. A backup head covers for layer 0 head 5: ablating L0 H5
      together with L0 H2 costs the same as ablating L0 H2 alone
      (redundancy 0.97), so the effect is carried by a group of layer-0 heads
      rather than by one head of 64, while L0 H5 with L1 H6 is additive
      ($+0.9591$ against a sum of $+0.908$), which rules out general
      fragility. The two-slice check is two offsets in one shard, not two
      corpora.
\item \textbf{Acts 0, 1 and 2.} The v1 headline (3.4525 against 3.0946 bits per
      character) is single-seed, 500 steps, and carried four self-declared
      confounds; it is superseded by Act 3 --- which is itself single-seed. The
      web interface has never been opened in a browser, so this paper makes no
      claim that it works.
\item \textbf{Scale, for everything.} The benchmark is small: 1.5M, 2.6M and
      6.3M-parameter models, a 128-token context, synthetic task families and
      C4-derived corpora. No claim in this paper generalises to models at
      scale, and none of it is intended to. % claims: T-01

\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

Every number in this paper is traceable to a tracked file. The audit trail for
each figure and each table --- the exact source file, the generating script,
the row count and the $n$ --- is in \texttt{report/MANIFEST.md}, and the
machine-readable claim ledger is \texttt{report/claims.csv}, whose
correspondence with the text is checked by \texttt{tools/check\_tex.py}.

The project is deterministic where it can be. Act 0's n-gram counts are
identical across seeds by construction, and the ledger records that fact rather
than hiding it behind a zero-width error bar. The Act 4 sweep reproduced its
2.6M rung's loss exactly from a different script on a different day. The Act 6
decoding sweep produced bit-identical quality and diversity values across a
repeat run. The 2.6M canonical checkpoint reproduced the MHA sweep loss of
2.8111 exactly. The three canonical checkpoints are exported as weights
(about 74 MB) and their SHA-256 digests, step counts and losses are pinned by
a test, so a reader can regenerate every Act~5 figure without retraining. A
fourth, the supervised fine-tune of the 6.3M model, is now exported in the
same format at
\texttt{checkpoints/canonical/6.3M-sft/model.pt}, which is what makes the
10c SFT-versus-base comparison possible.

Figures are never drawn by hand. Each one is produced by a committed script
under \texttt{src/eval/} that reads only the tracked results under
\texttt{results/}, and writes both a PNG and a vector PDF, so a figure cannot
drift from its result. The source data for every table in this paper is in
\texttt{report/tables/*.csv}. Two of the tables in Section~\ref{sec:act10a}
are \emph{not} transcribed by hand either: \texttt{tools/build\_act10\_tables.py}
writes the 10a headline-claims table and the 10b failure taxonomy from
\texttt{report/claims.csv} and from the defect records in
\texttt{docs/implementation.md}, both as a CSV and as a LaTeX fragment, and
this document reads those two fragments in with
\texttt{\textbackslash input}. The two generated fragments,
\texttt{report/tables/\allowbreak act10\_headline\_claims.tex} and
\texttt{report/tables/act10\_failure\_taxonomy.tex}, are part of the report
bundle and must travel with it.

The test suite is the implementation gate: 359 tests, 0 failures, 1 skipped.
The skip is a v1-era test that needs an artifact which is not present in a
fresh clone. The report itself is checked by a second gate, \texttt{tools/check\_tex.py},
because there is no local \texttt{pdflatex}: it verifies that every
\texttt{\textbackslash includegraphics} target resolves inside
\texttt{report/figures/} and has a PDF sibling, that every file in
\texttt{report/figures/} is either referenced by the report or listed with a
reason in the checker's explicit exclusion list, that environments and braces
balance, that every \texttt{\textbackslash label} is unique and every
\texttt{\textbackslash ref} resolves, that no absolute path or out-of-bundle
\texttt{\textbackslash input} appears, that inline math is balanced, and that
every claim in the text has a row in \texttt{report/claims.csv}. The checker
also carries a negative self-test (\texttt{--self-test}), which feeds it
deliberately broken copies of this document and asserts that it fails on each
of them: a gate that cannot fail is not a gate.
